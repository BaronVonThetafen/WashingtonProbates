Do I need probate in Washington?

Prepared by Ruth Apahidean West, Queen Anne Law, PLLC

Quick answer

Probate is generally necessary when someone must obtain court authority to collect, sell or transfer property belonging to a person who has died. Having a will does not, by itself, eliminate the need for probate.

Start with ownership, not the will

Make an inventory of bank accounts, real estate, vehicles and investments. Determine how each is titled and whether a named beneficiary or survivorship arrangement exists. A bank requesting letters testamentary is a strong signal that court appointment may be needed.

Consider alternatives

A surviving spouse may have access to jointly held funds or be able to rely on a community property agreement. Certain assets pass by beneficiary designation or trust. Qualifying personal property may be collected with a small estate affidavit.

Look at real estate separately

A house titled solely in the deceased person's name often requires someone with legal authority to sign a deed or sale documents. A transfer-on-death deed or joint tenancy may change the analysis.

Decide before filing

Gather the will, death certificate, title information and approximate balances. An attorney can identify which assets require court authority and which can transfer independently.

Common questions

Does a will avoid probate?

No. A will directs distribution and may nominate a representative, but does not itself confer the authority of court-issued letters.

Do all heirs have to agree to start probate?

Not necessarily. Appointment priority, notice and requests for nonintervention powers depend on the circumstances.

What if the bank says I need letters testamentary?

Ask what documents the bank accepts, then discuss court appointment or qualifying alternatives with counsel.
