Do I need probate in Washington?

Probate is generally necessary when someone must obtain court authority to collect, sell or transfer property belonging to a person who has died. Having a will does not, by itself, eliminate the need for probate.

Read guide →

How does probate work in Washington State?

Washington probate is a superior court process in which a personal representative or administrator receives authority to settle an estate: collect assets, address debts and taxes, distribute property and close the case.

Read guide →

Can I handle Washington probate if I live out of state?

A person living outside Washington may be able to serve as personal representative of a Washington estate by designating a qualified Washington resident agent and satisfying the other eligibility requirements. Living elsewhere does not automatically prevent appointment.

Read guide →

How do I sell or transfer an inherited house in Washington?

An inherited Washington house often requires probate if no other arrangement establishes who can transfer title. A will naming an executor is not the same as a court appointment authorizing that person to act.

Read guide →

How long does probate take in Washington?

There is no single duration for every Washington probate. Timing depends on obtaining authority, collecting property, resolving creditor claims, preparing taxes, distributing assets and completing the closing procedure.

Read guide →

What happens when someone dies without a will in Washington?

If a Washington resident dies without a will, the probate estate is distributed under Washington's intestate succession statutes. The court can appoint an administrator to collect assets, resolve debts and distribute property; the state does not automatically inherit everything.

Read guide →

Can I use a Washington small estate affidavit instead of probate?

Washington provides an affidavit procedure for qualifying personal property estates. The archived guide describes a $100,000 net probate-estate limit, a 40-day waiting period after death and notice to other successors. A house cannot be transferred using this affidavit.

Read guide →

Which assets pass outside probate in Washington?

Some property transfers at death through a beneficiary designation, trust, survivorship arrangement or other legal instrument rather than the will. Review the actual account or title documents before deciding whether court appointment is necessary.

Read guide →

How do I start probate in King County, Washington?

King County probate is administered through the superior court. An opening petition, supporting records, proposed order and required oath or bond are among the documents that may be needed. Local filing and hearing procedures should be checked before submitting paperwork.

Read guide →

What documents do I need to open Washington probate?

The necessary papers depend on whether there is a will, who will serve, whether advance notice is required and what authority the representative requests. An opening packet commonly includes a petition, proposed order, oath and relevant supporting documents.

Managing an estate

What are the duties of a Washington personal representative?

A personal representative must safeguard estate property, identify heirs and beneficiaries, provide notices, manage claims and taxes, keep records, distribute property properly and close the estate. Court appointment and nonintervention powers do not remove fiduciary duties.

Read guide →

Who needs notice after a Washington probate is opened?

Washington requires notice to interested persons and, in applicable cases, government agencies. The source guide identifies appointment notice, creditor-related notice, inventory requests and closing notices as separate obligations.

Read guide →

How do creditor claims work in Washington probate?

A representative must address estate debts. Publishing a notice to creditors may shorten claim periods, but publication alone is not a substitute for a reasonable search for identifiable creditors and the required direct notices.

Read guide →

How do I prepare an inventory of a Washington estate?

The estate inventory identifies property and values it as of the date of death. It is distinct from an accounting of later payments, sales and distributions. The archived guide describes a three-month preparation deadline after appointment.

Read guide →

What are nonintervention powers in Washington probate?

Nonintervention powers allow a court-appointed representative to manage many estate affairs without seeking a separate court order for each routine transaction. They do not eliminate duties to heirs, beneficiaries or creditors.

Read guide →

How do you close a Washington probate estate?

Closing generally follows settlement of debts and taxes, preparation of records and distribution of estate property. A nonintervention estate often uses a declaration of completion; other estates may require a court hearing and distribution order.

Read guide →

What taxes must be handled during Washington probate?

Estate administration may involve the decedent's final individual income tax return, an estate or trust income tax return, and Washington or federal estate tax returns. The applicable filings depend on the estate and the date of death.

Special issues

What changed in Washington probate law in June 2026?

The June 6, 2026 King County Probates article describes amendments effective June 11, 2026, including expanded intestate petition disclosures, eligibility restrictions and a longer appointment priority period. The changes should be checked against the enacted statutes before filing.

Read guide →

Can I administer an estate if I am not named executor in the will?

Someone not nominated in a will may be appointed as administrator with the will annexed if the nominated individuals cannot or do not serve and statutory appointment requirements are met.

Read guide →

What if I only have a copy of a Washington will?

A copy of a will may sometimes be admitted, but a missing original can raise a presumption of revocation. Establishing the terms, execution and circumstances of the missing document can require evidence and a court hearing.

Read guide →

Can a will that is not self-proving be admitted in Washington?

A will without an adequate self-proving witness affidavit is not necessarily invalid. It may need additional proof of execution before the court will admit it to probate.

Read guide →

What if a Washington probate beneficiary is a minor?

A minor or an adult unable to protect their own interests may need a court-appointed guardian ad litem in a probate. The appointment and scope depend on the circumstances and applicable rules.

Read guide →

What if a Washington estate owes more than it owns?

An insolvent estate has insufficient assets to pay its obligations. Relatives do not ordinarily become liable for a decedent's debts merely by inheriting, but handling estate assets or paying creditors incorrectly can create risks.

Read guide →

What rights does an unmarried partner have when someone dies in Washington?

An unmarried surviving partner may not have the same statutory inheritance protections as a spouse. Washington recognizes committed intimate relationships in certain property disputes, but a potential equitable property claim is different from an automatic right to inherit.
